1Key findings

Finding 1, the paper's central claim is robust Metropolitan status is the dominant correlate of accredited capacity, and this is identical with both indices. In the adjusted CMR model, metropolitan counties have about 8 times the expected number of accredited CMR facilities (IRR 8.23 with our index, IRR 8.66 with the external index, both P below 0.001).
Finding 2, deprivation adds nothing after rurality is modelled After adjustment for metropolitan status, neither index is associated with CMR or CCT capacity. With our index the CMR estimate moves from IRR 0.94 (P = 0.002) to IRR 0.98 (P = 0.43).
Finding 3, important, the unadjusted result is index dependent The validated external SDI does not reproduce our significant unadjusted CMR association. With the SDI there is no unadjusted association at all (IRR 1.01, P = 0.75). Our headline unadjusted number is therefore specific to how our index is built, not a general property of deprivation. This is explained fully in Section 4, and it strengthens rather than weakens the revised paper.
What this means for JACR The revised message is that deprivation indices should not be used as proxies for imaging access without adjusting for rurality. The external index makes that message stronger, because whether an unadjusted association appears at all depends on which deprivation index is chosen, while the metropolitan finding is the same with both. One sentence in the manuscript should be reframed to match this, and suggested wording is given in Section 3.

3Deliverable two, external validation with a published index

Our Economic Deprivation Index is built in house using principal component analysis of census indicators. A reviewer could argue that this construction produced the one significant result. To answer that directly we repeated the primary analysis with a completely independent measure, the Robert Graham Center Social Deprivation Index (SDI), 2015-2019 vintage. It is published, peer reviewed, and provided at the county level, so no aggregation was needed. It matched 3,133 of our 3,144 counties, and it agrees strongly with our index at the county level (Spearman rho 0.88).

The key result, stated plainly Our index shows a significant unadjusted CMR association. The validated external index shows no unadjusted association at all. Both indices agree completely once metropolitan status is in the model, and both show a metropolitan effect near 8 times for CMR. So the robust and reproducible message is metropolitan concentration, and the unadjusted deprivation number depends on the choice of index.
One sentence to reframe in the manuscript Any place that presents the unadjusted deprivation to CMR association as a real effect should be reframed as index specific. Suggested wording follows.
In unadjusted models our economic deprivation index was associated with lower CMR capacity (IRR 0.94, P = 0.002). This association was not reproduced by the externally validated Graham Center Social Deprivation Index (IRR 1.01, P = 0.75), and it was removed for our index after adjustment for metropolitan status (IRR 0.98, P = 0.43). The two indices, although strongly correlated (rho 0.88), differ in how closely they track rurality, and both agree that metropolitan status, not deprivation, is the dominant correlate of accredited capacity, with a metropolitan incidence rate ratio near 8 for CMR in each model.
This turns a likely reviewer criticism into a robustness result.

4Why the two indices differ, even though they agree at rho 0.88

The two indices rank counties similarly overall, so the puzzle is why they disagree in the raw CMR model. The reason is that they load on rurality very differently. Our index is dominated by income, poverty, unemployment, and education, which are concentrated in rural counties. The SDI adds dimensions that are high in dense urban cores, such as renter-occupied housing, crowding, no vehicle, and single-parent families. That makes the SDI far less tied to rurality. The numbers below show this directly.

Our index tracks rurality about 3.7 times more strongly than the SDI (0.249 divided by 0.068). Because the raw deprivation to CMR signal is really a rurality signal, the index that follows rurality more closely, our EDI, picks it up, while the more urban-balanced SDI does not. This is the same confounding the paper already describes, now shown to operate at the level of index construction itself.

5All calculations, step by step

The model in plain language

The outcome is the count of accredited facilities in a county. We fit a negative binomial regression. We add the natural log of the population of adults aged 45 and older as an offset. An offset turns a count model into a rate model, because it forces the count to be interpreted per person at risk. The predictor is the deprivation index divided by 10, so each unit is a 10-point rise on the 0 to 100 scale.

facility count follows negative binomial, with log(expected count) = b0 + b1 × (index / 10) + log(adults 45 and older)

The incidence rate ratio, or IRR, is the exponential of the coefficient b1. An IRR of 1 means no change. An IRR below 1 means fewer facilities as deprivation rises. An IRR above 1 means more facilities.

Turning an IRR into a percent change

The rule is percent change equals (IRR minus 1) times 100.

# Our EDI, CMR, unadjusted, IRR 0.9373 (0.9373 - 1) x 100 = -6.27 percent # about a 6.3 percent drop in CMR rate per 10 points # Our EDI, CMR, adjusted for metro, IRR 0.9828 (0.9828 - 1) x 100 = -1.72 percent # small and not significant, P = 0.4343 # External SDI, CMR, unadjusted, IRR 1.0064 (1.0064 - 1) x 100 = +0.64 percent # essentially zero, P = 0.7458 # Metropolitan status, CMR model, IRR 8.2307 metro counties have about 8.23 times the expected CMR count of nonmetro counties at the same deprivation and population # P = 4.43e-13

Agreement between the two indices

We ranked all counties by each index and compared the ranks. The Spearman rank correlation is 0.883 and the Pearson correlation is 0.882. Both are close to 1, which means the two indices order counties in almost the same way. This is what makes the disagreement in the unadjusted CMR model informative rather than random.

How strongly each index follows rurality

# Spearman correlation of each index with the rural code (higher code means more rural) EDI vs RUCC = +0.249 SDI vs RUCC = +0.068 ratio = 0.249 / 0.068 = 3.66 # our index follows rurality about 3.7 times more # Gap between nonmetro and metro mean index value EDI gap = 56.1 - 39.9 = 16.2 points SDI gap = 50.1 - 43.0 = 7.1 points

Descriptive check, mean deprivation by facility presence

Using the external SDI and the rate-eligible counties, the mean SDI is 48.7 in counties with no accredited facility and 44.8 in counties with at least one. The gap is small, only 3.9 points, which fits the finding that the SDI is weakly related to capacity. For comparison, our EDI showed a much larger gap of 52.9 versus 39.4, again because our index tracks rurality more strongly.

Sample sizes Models with our EDI use 3,029 rate-eligible counties. Models with the SDI use 3,027, a difference of 2 counties that lack an SDI value. Rate-eligible means at least 1,000 adults aged 45 and older, the same filter used throughout the study.

6How to reproduce everything

Every number and every figure in this document is produced by two committed scripts. Run them in order from the repository root. The first writes the results files, the second reads those results and draws the figures, so the tables and plots can never disagree.

# from the repository root, using the project environment pip install -r requirements.txt # step 1, run the analysis (writes the results json and txt) python code/09_validated_index_sdi.py # step 2, draw the two forest plots (reads the json) python code/10_jacr_forest_plots.py # outputs appear in output/jacr_revision/

The external SDI county file is committed at data/raw/rgc_sdi_2015_2019_county.csv, so the analysis runs offline. If that file is ever missing, the script downloads it once from the Robert Graham Center and saves it back to the same path. The software needed is Python 3.11 with pandas, numpy, scipy, statsmodels, and matplotlib, all listed in requirements.txt.

Reproducibility guarantee The models use a fixed specification, a fixed negative binomial dispersion of 1.0, and committed input data. Running the two scripts on a fresh clone reproduces every value in this report exactly.
